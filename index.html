What behaviour could a Land Value Tax encourage?

The tax is intended to do more than raise revenue. It changes the cost of holding valuable land.

Potential benefits

Less incentive to land-bank, more housing on valuable urban land, lower land prices over time, and less capital tied up purely in land appreciation.

Because the tax applies to land rather than the buildings on it, improving or adding buildings does not directly increase the tax simply because the improvement exists.

Potential trade-offs

Developers have a stronger incentive to maximise the number or value of dwellings on expensive sites. That can mean more housing — but it can also increase pressure toward smaller dwellings, cheaper builds, less private outdoor space, and loss of gardens or trees.

Housing quality therefore still depends on planning rules, building standards and what buyers will pay for.

Could landlords simply put the rent up?

Possibly, at least partly. A Citizen’s Income gives renters more disposable income, which can increase what a tight rental market is able to absorb.

But landlords cannot automatically pass their entire Land Value Tax bill through to tenants. Market rents are constrained by supply, demand and competing rentals, not just one landlord’s costs.

Land Value Tax vs Capital Gains Tax

Both can reduce the tax preference for property, but they operate very differently.

Land Value TaxCapital Gains Tax
What is taxed?Value of the landIncrease in value of an asset
When?Every yearGenerally when a taxable gain is realised

Key distinction: a CGT primarily taxes the return you make from owning an asset. An LVT taxes the land you continue to hold.

Methodology and limitations

Last updated: 29 August 2026

This tool is independent and is not an official Opportunity calculator. It models direct arithmetic for one scenario at a time. Nothing entered into the calculator is stored or transmitted.

AI was used to assist with research, coding and development of this calculator. Policy settings and calculation assumptions are documented here so they can be independently checked.

Current income tax uses New Zealand individual tax rates applying from 1 April 2025. Opportunity income tax is modelled at 28% to $50,000, 34% from $50,001 to $200,000, and 39% above $200,000.

The Citizen’s Income is modelled as $19,400 per eligible adult aged 18+. Land Value Tax is modelled at 1.75% for urban land and 0.5% for qualifying rural land.

NZ Super uses the published 2026 after-tax “M” rates. For testing, Jobseeker, Student Allowance, Supported Living Payment and Sole Parent Support can be entered as the actual current annual amount received after abatements. This avoids pretending to know every household-specific entitlement while the full benefit engine is still being developed.

Not modelled yet: Working for Families, Accommodation Supplement, disability supplements, ACC earners’ levy, KiwiSaver, student loan repayments, tax-credit interactions, precise Opportunity Super tax-adjustment rules for substantial other income, future rent/house-price changes, wages, interest rates, migration or behavioural responses.
