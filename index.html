What's the idea behind the Tax Reset?

For many New Zealanders, owning a home, commercial property or farm, has been one of the most reliable ways to build wealth. At the same time, getting onto the property ladder has become increasingly difficult for younger generations.

That raises some big questions:

How can we make homes more affordable for younger generations? Is it healthy for so much of our wealth to be tied up in land? And could we share the tax burden differently without unfairly penalising people who have spent a lifetime building what they have?

Opportunity's Tax Reset proposes one possible answer: a Citizen's Income for almost every adult, different income tax rates, and a tax on land.

Opportunity argues that this could make housing more affordable and encourage more investment outside property. Whether it would work, who would benefit, who would carry more of the cost, and whether that feels fair are much harder questions.

Understanding the Tax Reset

The result above is the arithmetic. These are the bigger ideas, trade-offs and questions behind it.

Where does the Citizen's Income money come from?

The $19,400 is not money created from nowhere. Opportunity says the Citizen's Income would be funded primarily through the Land Value Tax, alongside the wider changes in the Tax Reset.

Opportunity's modelling says the Land Value Tax would fund the Citizen's Income and leave around $4 billion a year for other investment. That is the party's projection, not an independently established outcome.

The useful question is not simply who receives $19,400, but how the package redistributes money once the Citizen's Income, income tax and Land Value Tax are considered together.

Isn't this just a giant money-go-round?

To an extent, yes. Under the Tax Reset, most adults would receive a Citizen's Income while many of those same people would also pay income tax - and some would pay Land Value Tax.

That might seem unnecessarily circular. The reason for making the payment universal is to avoid having to determine who is "poor enough" to receive it, and to remove the steep benefit abatements that can make earning more financially unrewarding.

For some people, much of the Citizen's Income will effectively be offset by the tax they pay. For others, it will represent a significant increase in disposable income.

The important number isn't simply the $19,400 payment. It's the net result after the Citizen's Income, income tax and any Land Value Tax are all taken into account.

Would a Citizen's Income simplify the welfare system?

Potentially - but it wouldn't eliminate administration.

Because almost every eligible adult receives the same Citizen's Income regardless of earnings, some of the income testing, benefit abatements and eligibility administration associated with the current system could disappear.

But the wider Tax Reset still requires administration. Some people would need additional support or top-ups, while Land Value Tax would require land valuations, classifications, exemptions and deferrals. Income tax would also continue to operate.

The system may shift where the administrative complexity sits rather than simply making it disappear.

What behaviour could a Land Value Tax encourage?

The tax is intended to do more than raise revenue. It changes the cost of holding valuable land.

Potential benefits

Less incentive to land-bank, more housing on valuable urban land, lower land prices over time, and less capital tied up purely in land appreciation.

Because the tax applies to land rather than the buildings on it, improving or adding buildings does not directly increase the tax simply because the improvement exists.

Potential trade-offs

Developers have a stronger incentive to maximise the number or value of dwellings on expensive sites. That can mean more housing - but it can also increase pressure toward smaller dwellings, cheaper builds, less private outdoor space, and loss of gardens or trees.

Housing quality therefore still depends on planning rules, building standards and what buyers will pay for.

What if land and house prices fall?

Lower land prices are an intended effect of the policy. Opportunity projects that a 1.75% Land Value Tax could reduce property prices by around 10-15% as the market adjusts.

That could make buying a home easier for future buyers, but it also means existing owners could see the value of their property and their equity fall. The effect could be particularly important for people who bought recently with a large mortgage, because a significant fall in value could leave some owners with little equity or, in some cases, owing more than the property is worth.

This calculator does not attempt to predict future property values or changes in household equity. Opportunity proposes a decade-long transition, so any price adjustment would occur alongside the policy phase-in rather than necessarily happening all at once.

What about farmers and other land-rich, cash-poor owners?

Land Value Tax is based on the value of land, not the income or profit that land produces. That matters for farms and other owners who may hold valuable land without having a similarly large annual cash income.

Opportunity proposes a lower 0.5% rate for qualifying rural land, exemptions for privately owned conservation land, and the ability for farmers to defer payments during difficult weather or economic seasons. Retirees would also be able to defer Land Value Tax, with payment ultimately coming from their estate.

Deferral changes when the tax is paid, rather than necessarily removing the liability. The calculator shows the annual tax at the applicable rate but does not model individual exemptions or deferral arrangements.

How is Land Value Tax different from Capital Gains Tax?

Both can reduce the tax preference for property, but they operate very differently.

Could this actually become policy?

Opportunity would first need to win enough support to enter Parliament. It would then need sufficient influence in government or confidence-and-supply negotiations to persuade other parties to support the Tax Reset.

Even if Opportunity becomes part of the next government's governing arrangements, that doesn't mean the Tax Reset would become law exactly as proposed. Tax rates, the Citizen's Income, exemptions, the transition period or other parts of the package could all change through negotiation and the legislative process.

This calculator models Opportunity's published 2026 proposal - not a prediction of what a future government will enact.

What about overseas ownership?

Opportunity appears to be leaving New Zealand's existing overseas-investment and foreign-buyer rules unchanged. Its published 2026 policies do not set out a separate proposal to expand or further restrict overseas ownership of New Zealand land.

What might happen next?

A calculator can compare tax settings, but it cannot reliably predict how millions of people, businesses and markets would respond to them.

The Tax Reset could affect property prices, housing development, rents, wages, employment choices, investment, saving and inflation. Some of those changes are deliberate aims of the policy. Others could offset or amplify the numbers shown here.

Opportunity argues that the package would encourage housing development and productive investment, improve workers' bargaining power and make housing more affordable. It also acknowledges the possibility of some inflationary pressure. Those are policy arguments and projections - the eventual effects would depend on how people, markets, government and the Reserve Bank responded.

For that reason, the result above should be read as a comparison of the policy settings, not a forecast of your future financial position.

What this calculator does and doesn't calculate

Last updated: 29 August 2026

This tool is independent and is not an official Opportunity calculator. It models direct arithmetic for one scenario at a time. Nothing entered into the calculator is stored or transmitted.

AI was used to assist with research, coding and development of this calculator. Policy settings and calculation assumptions are documented here so they can be independently checked.

Current income tax uses New Zealand individual tax rates applying from 1 April 2025. Opportunity income tax is modelled at 28% to $50,000, 34% from $50,001 to $200,000, and 39% above $200,000.

The Citizen’s Income is modelled as $19,400 per eligible adult aged 18+. Land Value Tax is modelled at 1.75% for urban land and 0.5% for qualifying rural land. These are the fully implemented rates; Opportunity proposes phasing the Tax Reset in over 10 years.

NZ Super uses the published 2026 after-tax “M” rates. For testing, Jobseeker, Student Allowance, Supported Living Payment and Sole Parent Support can be entered as the actual current annual amount received after abatements. This avoids pretending to know every household-specific entitlement while the full benefit engine is still being developed.

KiwiSaver 2.0 is not currently included in the calculation. Opportunity's wider Tax Reset also proposes changes to KiwiSaver, including progressively increasing employee and employer contributions to 6% each. Employee contributions could affect take-home pay, although that money remains the individual's retirement savings. The result shown here therefore models the direct effect of the Citizen's Income, income tax and Land Value Tax - not every component of the wider Tax Reset.

Not modelled yet: Working for Families, Accommodation Supplement, disability supplements, ACC earners’ levy, KiwiSaver, student loan repayments, tax-credit interactions, precise Opportunity Super tax-adjustment rules for substantial other income, future rent/house-price changes, wages, interest rates, migration or behavioural responses.
